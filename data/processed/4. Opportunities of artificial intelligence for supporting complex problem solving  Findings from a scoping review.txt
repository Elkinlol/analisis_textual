A B S T R A C T

The research objective of this paper is to advance knowledge about the role of artificial intelligence (AI) in complex problem-solving. A problem is complex due to the 
large number of highly inter-connected variables affecting the problem state. Complex problem-solving situations often change decremental or worsen, forcing a 
problem solver to act immediately, under considerable time pressure. While research findings support the assumption that (1) affective, (2) (meta-)cognitive, and (3) 
social processes support complex problem-solving, opportunities of AI for supporting complex problem-solving need to be further investigated. This article presents a 
scoping review of relevant literature from the last five years. The study included, N = 38 studies for coding and analysis. Our findings show that in addition to the 
increased number of publications, the current trend suggests increased quality of published work. Human-AI collaboration in complex problem-solving has been 
explored across a broad variety of AI application domains. However, the four dimensions of complex problem – namely, cognitive, metacognitive, social and affective 
- have been augmented by AI to a different extent. Although most of the work has been done in the cognitive domain, it is encouraging to see progress across social 
and affective dimensions as well. Implications for future research and practice are being discussed.

1. Introduction

Complex problem-solving involves engaging with problems that are 
dynamic, could have multiple solutions, and usually require multidis­
ciplinary teamwork (D¨orner, 1980). These are “problems that resemble 
real-life situations” (Kunze et al., 2018, p. 3). As society has become 
more globalized and interconnected, the problems generated have 
become more complex and intractable, raising the need for skills to 
engage with new challenges and also a nuanced understanding of how to 
approach them. Fortunately, the nature of solving complex problems has 
changed over the last several decades, due to advances in computer 
science, artificial intelligence (AI), and cognitive psychology. Specif­
ically, unprecedented amounts of data, advanced algorithms, and 
significantly improved computation and distributed infrastructure (such 
as managed data lakes) now support collaboration across diverse teams.

dynamic problem system in its entirety, imagining the events that would 
take place if a particular action were to be performed (de Laat et al.,

Solving complex problems, therefore, depends on the ability of in­
dividuals to integrate diverse background knowledge and expertise into

Research on collaborative and cooperative learning and work demon­
strates the importance of social and cognitive processes in creating a 
common understanding and shared conception of the problem and 
possible solutions (Van Den Bossche et al., 2011). Group work and 
complex problem-solving are not detached from emotions and depend 
on each individual’s motivation and abilities to plan and monitor their 
activities (Hermes & Stelling, 2016). The research in group and collab­
orative problem-solving details the importance of understanding the 
interplay of factors that influence how individuals and groups regulate 
affective and motivational processes (Hermes & Stelling, 2016).

successful learning, work, and life in general. Today’s learners and

primarily used to support human capabilities. Current research and 
practice in AI mainly focuses cognitive support by processing large

complex problem-solving at the level of affective, (meta-)cognitive and

AI systems are capable of analysing large datasets, including un­
structured data, in real-time, and detecting patterns or structures that 
can be used to support human decision-making and team-based prob­
lem-solving. In the context of education, AI has the potential to trans­
form the way that learners and educators approach complex problems, 
by providing personalized insights and data-driven recommendations. 
When AI is used in complex problem solving, the patterns of interaction 
become more sophisticated, since AI teammates augment human con­
tributions and can influence the resulting actions by humans. This in­
fluence of human-AI teaming increases the importance of social, 
affective, cognitive, and metacognitive aspects to problem-solving. Un­
fortunately, a substantial gap exists in the literature regarding how these 
attributes are expressed in joint human-AI teams in complex settings. 
This paper presents the findings of a scoping literature review focusing 
on empirical evidence on how artificial intelligence supports human 
complex problem-solving and the nature of human-AI collaboration in 
complex problem-solving at the level of (meta-)cognitive and social 
practices, as well as affective processes.

2. Research on problem-solving

3. Opportunities of artificial intelligence for complex problem- 
solving

Research on problem-solving has a long tradition in both psychology 
and education. Jonassen (1997) classifies problems on a continuum 
from well-structured to ill-structured. Well-structured problems have a 
well-defined initial state, a known goal state or solution, and a con­
strained set of known procedures for solving a class of problems. In 
contrast, the solutions to ill-structured problems are neither predictable 
nor convergent because they often possess unknown aspects. Addition­
ally, they possess multiple solutions or solution strategies or often no 
solutions at all (Funke, 2012). Jonassen (2010) reiterates that the 
structure of a problem often overlaps with complexity: Ill-structured 
problems tend to be more complex, especially those emerging from 
everyday practice, whereas most well-structured problems tend to be 
less complex. The complexity of a problem is determined by the number 
of functions or variables involved; the degree of connectivity among 
these variables; the type of functional relationships between these 
properties; and the stability of the properties of the problem over time 
(Mainert et al., 2019). Simple problems are composed of a few variables, 
while ill-structured (complex) problems may include many variables 
that may interact in unpredictable ways. When the conditions of a 
problem change, a person must continuously adapt his or her under­
standing of the problem while searching for new solutions, because the 
old solutions may no longer be viable. Static problems are those in which 
the factors are stable over time while ill-structured problems tend to be 
more dynamic (Seel et al., 2009).

Building on the previous work by Andrews-Todd and Forsyth (2020), 
Salas et al. (2008), Barth and Funke (2010), D¨orner and Funke (2017), 
and Spering et al. (2005), among others, we posit that complex problem 
solving encompasses three interrelated domains of (meta-)cognitive, 
affective, and social processes. Although there is an abundance of

Given the rather heterogenous nature (i.e., draws on various fields 
and disciplined) of the research on human-machine collaboration and 
complex problem-solving, we opted for a scoping review, instead of a 
more precise systematic literature review. In so doing, we still followed 
the guidelines for a scientifically rigorous literature review, except that 
we searched only one digital database – i.e., Scopus, following an 
approach similar to Cetindamar et al. (2022). In general, scoping re­
views are commonly used for “reconnaissance” - to clarify working 
definitions and conceptual boundaries of a topic or field. Scoping re­
views are therefore particularly useful when a body of literature has not 
yet been comprehensively reviewed or exhibits a complex or heteroge­
neous nature not amenable to a more precise systematic review of the 
evidence. Moreover, compared to other databases, such as Web of Sci­
ence, Scopus also includes conference papers that are crucial for 
emerging fields such as human-AI collaboration. Compared to Google 
Scholar, on the other hand, Scopus includes less grey literature, and it 
has been widely used in bibliometric studies (Caputo & Kargina, 2022; 
Tiban´a-Herrera et al., 2018).

A general assumption in problem-solving research is that the first 
thing a person does when confronted with a problem is to try to 
construct a mental representation of its relevant features (Seel et al., 
2009). This internal representation of a problem is termed a problem 
space – and if its construction has been successful the problem space will 
consist of information about the initial and goal state of the problem as 
well as information about the operators which can be applied to solve it. 
More specifically, Rumelhart et al. (1986) argue that these internal 
functions during problem-solving are dependent on two interacting 
modules or sets of units: (1) schemata and (2) mental models. The 
resulting cognitive architecture corresponds to a great extent with Pia­
get’s epistemology (Piaget, 1976) and its basic mechanisms of assimi­
lation and accommodation (Ifenthaler & Seel, 2011). Assimilation is 
dependent on the availability and activation of schemata, which allow 
new information to be integrated immediately into pre-existing cogni­
tive structures. If a schema does not fit immediately with the re­
quirements of a new task it can be adjusted to meet them using 
accretion, tuning, or reorganization (Seel et al., 2009). Accordingly, if a 
schema for any problem type is available, the schema is mapped onto the 
problem to be solved promptly (Jonassen, 2000). If assimilation is not 
successful, accommodation must take place to reorganise or restructure 
an individual’s knowledge. However, when no schema is available or its 
reorganization fails, the human mind switches to the construction of a 
mental model which is defined as a dynamic ad hoc representation of a 
phenomenon or problem that aims at creating subjective plausibility 
through simplifying and envisioning the situation, or through analogical 
reasoning. From a meta-cognitive perspective, the above-mentioned 
processes may be monitored and reflected for advancing these func­
tions for future events (Azevedo et al., 2013).

Finally, shared mental models are regarded as a significant factor for 
successful problem-solving (Cannon-Bowers & Salas, 2001). However, 
the concept of shared mental model is used and interpreted differently 
by various disciplines, for example, industrial and organisational psy­
chology, human factors, social psychology, or system dynamics (Bad­
ke-Schaub et al., 2011). Previous research on social processing in 
complex problem-solving shows that if humans share similar individual 
mental models they are more effective in their problem-solving perfor­
mance (Van Den Bossche et al., 2011). For example, Lim and Klein 
(2006) found that shared task knowledge and shared team knowledge 
were valid predictors for problem-solving performance. Similar findings 
regarding the influence of shared task and team knowledge on 
problem-solving performance were found in a series of studies using 
flight simulators in laboratory settings (Mathieu et al., 2005).

We intentionally opted for “problem-solving” as a basis for our 
search, rather than “complex problem-solving”, to capture a broader set 
of studies, as there might be a difference in conceptualisations between 
ill-defined and well-defined problems across diverse fields of research. 
We limited our search to include studies published over a five year 
period, starting in 2017 resulting in 2607 studies. After removing du­
plicates, we obtained 2597 studies considered for screening. The 
screening process was conducted by two researchers who read the title 
and the abstract for each of the studies, resulting in 809 studies 
considered for inclusion. Both researchers have been actively exploring 
the domain of human-AI collaboration with previous work being done in 
developing models for complex skills assessment, such as creativity, 
critital chinking or problem solving. Given the complexity of the topic, 
two researchers skimmed all 809 studies and reviewed them for inclu­
sion or exclusion, discussing every decision until a common agreement 
was met. The inclusion criteria was defined around the following prin­
ciples: only studies that are relevant to the research question and pub­
lished as peer-reviewed journal articles, conference proceedings, books, 
or reports will be included; only studies published in English will be 
included in this scoping review; studies published from 2017 to 2021 
were considered for inclusion; studies must include human participants, 
regardless of age, gender, ethnicity, or geographical location. The level 
of agreement between coders was generally high, with an accuracy rate 
exceeding 95%. However, there were some instances where the coders 
disagreed, particularly in cases where the studies employed different 
tools or simulations to facilitate teamwork. All disagreements were 
resolved through discussion and consensus among the coders. The ma­
jority of the studies that were excluded were either focused on devel­
oping an AI algorithm, rather than human-machine teaming, were not 
focusing on supporting humans in complex problem-solving, or were not 
empirical studies (e.g., an opinion paper discussing the nature of human- 
AI collaboration in complex problem solving). The final dataset included 
38 studies selected for coding and analysis. The list of selected papers 
has been explicitly specifically marked in the reference list.

4.2. Analysis

While research findings support the assumption that (1) affective, (2) 
(meta-)cognitive, and (3) social processes support complex problem- 
solving, opportunities of AI for supporting complex problem-solving 
need to be further investigated [18]. Thus, the central research objec­
tive of this paper is to advance knowledge about the role of AI in com­
plex problem-solving. To guide the scoping review, the following 
research question was formulated: How may AI complement humans 
during complex problem-solving through the functions of (1) affective 
processing, (2) cognitive and meta-cognitive processing, as well as (3) 
social processing?

paper, coded all 38 studies based on the following dimensions.

5. Results and discussion

5.1. Summary of key publications

Although research interests in AI, Human-Computer Interaction 
(HCI), and machine learning represent the dominant focus of authors of 
included publications, only nine studies included collaboration between 
authors who explicitly labelled their research interest in at least two out 
of those three research areas (Fig. 5). On the other hand, 18 publications 
had at least one author with research interests in AI, HCI or machine 
learning, whereas 11 publications had no authors with expertise in these 
three areas (Fig. 5). Other dominant research domains include computer 
vision, engineering design and human-robot interaction, whereas 
several authors explicitly stated their research interests in neuroscience, 
robotics and social robotics (Fig. 5). Finally, at least one publication 
included authors with research interests in research domains such as 
education, explanation-aware computing, innovation and arts, legal 
psychology, ethics, management, sport, tourism or immunology, to 
name a few.

5.2. Dimensions of human-AI collaboration

Although the number of publications in the area of human-AI 
collaboration dropped in 2018 and 2019, it seems that over the last 
two years, this topic continued to receive considerable attention (Fig. 1, 
left side). In addition to the increased number of publications, the cur­
rent trend suggests increased quality of published work. Specifically, 
Fig. 1 (left side) shows a sustained increase in the number of journal 
publications since 2018. Moreover, it is also noteworthy that recent 
publications tend to utilize a considerably larger sample size (Fig. 1, 
right side). While early publications in this domain either did not report 
sample size (or it was not relevant – e.g., conceptual papers), over the 
last two years, it seems that recent contributions tend to be research 
focused with large numbers of participants.

The notion of trust has been identified as one of the driving mech­

symbiosis between humans and machines as they interact in complex 
problem-solving. This approach, that involves fixed division of roles, 
neglects the emergent character of interaction and the negotiation of 
roles and tasks during complex problem solving. For effective augmen­
tation of human intelligence by machines, the roles of each must emerge 
through interaction.

It is important to note that study of human-machine teams involves 
studying interactions between two dynamic systems, that spans across 
four domains – cognitive, metacognitive, affective, and social. That is, 
humans will adapt what they do as they learn to make better use of what 
the AI has to offer. Correspondingly, the AI will (over longer periods of 
time) learn to do a better job of understanding and meeting the needs of 
the human team members. In other words, the sheer complexity of 
research on human-machine teaming stems from the fact that we are 
observing two dynamic systems, because both parts (human and AI) will 
be changing. We have no reason to believe that what either of the 
partners in human-machine teams does – and therefore how they 
interact – will stabilise. Future research needs to provide a compre­
hensive description of relevant cognitive, social, and affective di­
mensions, and how these unfold throughout the problem-solving 
process. The outcomes of this potential work under this domain need to 
identify patterns of human-AI interaction profiles in team problem- 
solving interactions to inform the development of guidelines for the 
effective use of AI in team problem-solving interactions.

tionship between these four constructs, let alone how this impacts joint 
human-AI teams. Despite extensive literature around distributed 
cognition, teamwork, and problem solving (Canan et al., 2017; D¨orner & 
Funke, 2017; Hutchins, 2006), no study to date attempts to theorise the 
interplay between human and artificial collaboration in complex 
problem-solving at the level of (meta-)cognitive and social practices and 
affect. This perhaps comes as no surprise, as AI research is yet to produce 
technology that is capable of fully engaging in the cognitive (e.g., a 
shared mental model), and social (e.g., trust and relational) processes 
needed for sensemaking in solving real-world problems. As argued by 
Klein et al. (2005), to become a “real” team player (on par with humans) 
AI needs to be able to: i) engage in common ground activities; ii) be 
reasonably predictable and able to predict actions of others; iii) engage 
in goal negotiation; iv) coordinate activities with humans; v) interpret 
and send intention signals; and vi) control the costs of coordinated ac­
tivity. Although rapid progress is being made in computer science and 
related fields towards these goals, a key deficit militating against such 
advancements relates to the lack of research looking into how humans 
collaborate with AI in sensemaking. For instance, in their recent study 
Gyory et al. (2021) analysed discourse data to show that communication 
in AI-assisted teams was richer, more diverse and more frequent, 
compared to teams that did not have AI assistance. While this work 
represents an important contribution to the development of science of 
human-machine teaming, the study was conducted in a structured and 
specifically designed environment that allows for a limited number of 
team dynamics to be observed (Gyory et al., 2021). This proposal posits 
that theory and a rigorous conceptual framework are necessary pre­
cursors to technical development particularly in social domains, such as 
human learning or complex problem-solving.

To sum up, human-machine collaboration in complex problem- 
solving is a rapidly growing area of research that has the potential to 
significantly improve the efficiency and effectiveness of problem-solving 
in a wide range of practical applications. Some key areas of focus for 
research in this area include.

When it comes to educational research and practice, a critical theme 
going forward will be the design of assessment, which drives teacher and 
student behaviour. More than 30 years ago, Salomon and his colleagues 
(1991) raised the issue of whether to evaluate the performance of the 
student/machine system as a whole or just the student after the AI is 
removed. The emergence of generative AI has prompted educational 
institutions to urgently review their assessment design, as AI writing 
applications are now capable of producing polished writing that can 
partially or fully answer writing assignments across various fields, 
provided with the appropriate prompts. Although designing with 
humans-in-the-loop is often praised as a principle, this assumes that 
humans are competent. However, there is currently little evidence that 
students are capable of engaging critically with generative AI applica­
tions. To make effective use of generative, conversational AI, this 
knowledge gap must be filled rapidly, building on prior research in 
automated writing evaluation, conversational user interfaces, and 
pedagogical agents.

6. Conclusions and future research

1. Developing more advanced machine learning and artificial intelli­
gence techniques to enable machines to better understand and 
interpret human inputs, and to more effectively collaborate with 
humans in problem-solving tasks.  
2. Investigating new forms of human-machine interaction, such as 
natural language processing and gesture-based interfaces, to make it 
easier for humans to interpret, communicate with and control 
machines.  
3. Developing new methods for evaluating the performance of human- 
machine teams, in order to better understand the strengths and 
weaknesses of different forms of collaboration and to identify areas 
for improvement.  
4. Exploring the ethical and social implications of human-machine 
collaboration, in order to ensure that these systems are developed 
and deployed in ways that are fair and beneficial to all stakeholders.  
5. Applying human-machine collaboration to real-world complex 
problems in fields such as healthcare, finance, transportation, and 
environmental management, as well as education.

Overall, human-machine collaboration in complex problem-solving 
has the potential to rejuvenise how researchers and practicioners 
approach and solve some of the most pressing challenges facing society 
today.
