1 | INTRODUCTION

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has brought transformative changes in education, offering innovative tools and ap-

proaches that can enhance teaching and learning (Chakravorti, 2022; Gill et al., 2023; Grubaugh & Levitt, 2023).

AI in education promises to support students with special needs, reduce school dropout, personalise learning,

support decision-­making, generate feedback, assess new skills, etc. (Bentley et al., 2018; Brundage, 2019; Vincent-­

Lancrin & van der Vlies, 2020). Additionally, AI in education (AIED) brings a fresh set of competencies that edu-

cators should cultivate. The rapid advancement of AI technologies requires an understanding of AI capabilities

and limitations. This entails more than just basic digital literacy; it encompasses data literacy, algorithm literacy,

computational thinking and design thinking. Notably, the ethical use of AI in educational settings becomes a

paramount concern, making AI and data ethics a crucial aspect of teacher competence (Berendt et al., 2020; U.S.

Department of Education, Office of Educational Technology, 2023; European Commission, 2022).

Even as schools increasingly embrace AI tools, teacher preparation is not prioritised and is experiencing delays.

A recent UNESCO global survey, which included over 450 schools and universities, revealed that fewer than 10%

of them have established institutional policies or formal guidance regarding the use of generative AI applications

(UNESCO survey, 2023).

The rise of AI in education has provided food for discussion on teachers' AI competencies in recent litera-

ture.2020 Trujillo-­Cabezas (2020) proposed AI digital competencies for educators, including the utilisation of

applications, adept information management, content creation for learning and facilitating student connections

through technology. Teachers require both knowledge and tools to evaluate the appropriateness of AI-­supported

activities in achieving their educational objectives and transform their way of teaching (Flogie & Krabonja, 2023).

Liu and Li (2022) defined teacher human capital for AI in education through three facets: expertise and proficiency

in AI in education, emotional disposition and attitudes relevant to AI in education, and ethical considerations for

using AI in education. Xu (2020) underscores educators developing AI competency for transformative roles, while

Markauskaite et al. (2022) support integrating digital technologies and gaining hands-­on AI experience.

Despite the growing interest in AI competencies, the existing literature highlights a significant gap in well-­

defined frameworks for educators to acquire competencies essential for using AI to the benefit of their learners.

Ng et al. (2021a) emphasise the limited exploration within current studies of enhancing teachers' AI digital com-

petency through teacher education programmes. Polak et al. (2022) conclude that, while examining various AI

frameworks, the development of AI competencies involves a broad spectrum of skills, knowledge and attitudes.

While some are specific to AI, others are adapted versions of digital competencies or essential competencies

irrespective of technology use (such as problem-­solving and contextual consideration). Ng et al. (2022) reflect on

three educational theories; Bloom's taxonomy to view cognition levels of AI literacy education, the TPACK model

to understand how educators should learn to equip their students with AI literacy (Ng et al., 2021b), and the P21's

Framework to identify the four types of digital competencies in the 21st century. AI digital competencies, as out-

lined by the P21's Framework for 21st Century Learning (National Research Council, 2012), recognise AI literacy

as a vital skill within a comprehensive framework. This perspective emphasises its role in fostering non-­technical,

critical and complex literacies. Regardless of these early attempts to analyse the AI CFTs, the emergence of new AI

frameworks highlights challenges in understanding their purpose and utilising and adapting them for professional

development programmes.

Currently, there are several critical issues related to AI CFTs that have been not addressed by previous study

of Ng et al. (2022) (see, Table 1). First, there might be a scattered landscape of various AI CFTs, making it difficult

for educators, researchers and policymakers to find and compare them efficiently. Second, different AI CFTs

might exhibit varying levels of quality and effectiveness in preparing educators to utilise AI. Third, there might

be a need for a deeper understanding of how AI CFTs are designed and how they can practically be incorporated

into teacher education programmes. Finally, educators might not feel adequately prepared to leverage AI in their

teaching practices due to a limited understanding of relevant frameworks.

Therefore, this critical review addresses problematic issues related to the effective integration of AI in ed-

ucation. The review represents the initial effort to compile teachers' AI competence frameworks, analyse their

strengths and weaknesses, and categorise them to enhance their practicality for researchers, educators and pol-

icymakers. Exploring various AI CFTs may help to understand their design and characteristics, and facilitate their

integration into teacher education enhancing the preparedness of educators in harnessing AI potential within their

teaching practices.

The paper explores the new AI CFTs that emerged in the recent educational literature, analyses them within

the Competence Construct Claim (Child & Shaw, 2023), and classifies groups of AI CFTs into five distinct types: (1)

Existing competence model-­oriented; (2) Competence areas/domains-­oriented; (3) process-­driven (process com-

petency model); (4) AI systems-­driven (specific work/job model); and (5) Competence level-­driven (multidimen-

sional). Each type of CFT reflects a specific underlying approach to modelling teachers' competencies and how

these models could effectively be applied to prepare teachers for AI in education. The paper highlights several

pertinent questions and challenges educators face when they want to embrace AI in their practices; it considers

the importance of theoretical models in the successful integration of AI in education and the interplay between

subject-­specific knowledge and AI proficiency.

1.1 | Research objective of the review

•	 To explore and classify AI CFTs into distinct types or approaches within Competence Construct Claims cat-

egory (Child & Shaw, 2023). This objective seeks to provide a structured framework for understanding the

definitions, labels and rationale of different AI CFTs.

The research questions

The following questions guided the literature review:

RQ 1: Which AI CFTs are available in recent educational literature?

RQ 2: How can AI CFTs be classified within Competence Construct Claims? What are underlying approaches,

focus areas and characteristics of these AI CFTs?

To address the research objectives and research questions, we searched for the AI CFTs in scientific litera-

ture online in Scopus and Web of Science with the key words ‘Artificial Intelligence’, ‘Competence framework’ or

‘Competence models’, ‘for teachers’ or ‘for educators’. Next, the papers were screened to check their suitability

for the topic and analysed according to the Construct Claim (Child & Shaw, 2023).

By addressing these research objectives and research questions, this study aimed to provide a classified over-

view of AI competence frameworks that are designed to equip teachers with essential skills. Furthermore, it high-

lights the existing challenges in these frameworks, thereby contributing to the ongoing dialogue on how educators

can best prepare for AI in education.

2 | EDUCATORS HAVE CHALLENGES IN INTEGRATION AI 
IN EDUCATION

Educators are one of the key actors in the integration of AI in education in the present revolutionary era of

Industry 4.0 (Felix, 2020). This era is marked by ‘connecting everything to everything everywhere’ – a vast inter-

connected network of devices, autonomous vehicles, smartphones and smart systems designed for sensing, pre-

dicting, decision-­making and interaction with the physical world (Rothman & Gulli, 2022, p. 12). Teachers, being

pivotal in preparing the next generation for an AI-­integrated future, play a crucial role in fostering willingness and

(Ayanwale et al., 2022). The success of AI education depends on teacher readiness, as the teaching and learning

process requires their central involvement (Trotsko et al., 2019). According to ethical guidelines developed by

(European Commission, 2022), it is important for educators to understand the basic mechanisms and limitations of

AI systems and how AI systems can be used to support teaching and learning in a safe and ethical way.

AI has the potential to support teachers and improve the educational experience for students. It encompasses var-

ious areas such as AI instruction, intelligent systems, adaptive learning and gamification (Hwang et al., 2022; Zawacki-­

Richter et al., 2019; Zhai et al., 2021). Bryant et al. (2020) suggested that 20 to 40 percent of current teacher time spent

on activities could be automated using existing technology to save teacher time. For example, AI real-­time analytics

tools can help teachers monitor and assist students using AI-­based tutoring software for self-­paced learning (Aleven

et al., 2022). Additionally, AI systems can assist teachers by automatically tracking and recognising students' motions and

behaviours, providing valuable insights for personalised guidance (Felix, 2020). AI technologies can also tailor education

to students' needs and automate routine tasks, allowing teachers more time for creativity (Chikobava & Romeike, 2021).

AI systems can do automated, repetitive task and design, that empower learning experiences, creativity, thinking, solu-

tions of problems, collaboration and they will augment teachers' roles. Supporters of AI in education stress that AI will

perform repetitive tasks and pattern recognition, which are constantly done by teachers (Arora, 2021).

The integration of AI technologies into educational contexts requires not only teachers' digital skills but AI

competencies as well (Sostero & Tolan, 2022; Williamson & Eynon, 2020). Educators' AI competencies include a

range of skills that empower teachers to critically evaluate AI technologies, effectively engage with AI, and employ

AI tools in diverse settings. According to Edwards et al. (2018), educators need the competence to choose and

create instructions aligned with machine learning, track students' advancement and offer emotional and cognitive

assistance through robotics powered by artificial intelligence.

However, equipping teachers with AI competencies seems to lag behind the optimism around the use of AI in daily

educational practice. Many teachers feel overwhelmed by the pressure to integrate AI into their teaching without

fully understanding its workings and impact (especially with recent rapid developments in the field of Large Language

Models). AI concepts (Computer vision, machine learning, deep learning, natural language processing, etc.) tend to

be abstract and complex and are hard to understand for teachers (Kim & Kwon, 2023; Long & Magerko, 2020); there

are scarce AI professional development opportunities for teachers, very few institutional policies or formal guidance

on the use of generative AI applications and inadequate guidance on the necessary competencies for educators in

academic literature and existing educational policies (Ayanwale et al., 2022; UNESCO, 2023).

To empower educators with AI competencies, it is essential to delineate a targeted set of knowledge domains

and skills, systematically arranged within a competency framework or model. The goal of this AI competency

framework for educators is to articulate fundamental proficiencies (Knowledge, skills and abilities) essential for

teachers. This competency framework can serve as a tool for program developers, and learning coordinators

involved in the program development process, from developing early ideas to implementation (Blok et al., 2021).

For successful integration of AI in education, teachers, as primary stakeholders in education and in continuous

interaction with their learners, should be engaged in an early phase. As practical guidance on how to equip teach-

ers with AI competencies is lacking, a first step may be to develop a framework or model that aligns AI competen-

cies necessary to use AI to the benefit of learners.

Then we will discuss AI CFTs classified according to five categories: (1) Existing competence model-­oriented; (2)

Competence areas/domains-­oriented; (3) process-­driven (process competency model); (4) AI systems-­driven (spe-

cific work/job model) and (5) a competence level-­driven (multidimensional).

The educational approach to defining competencies focuses on skill development, achieving standards and

obtaining credentials. A competence can be narrowly defined as an action, behaviour, demonstrated outcome or

minimum standard, with varying levels of mastery articulated through different statements (Bourke et al., 1975

cited in Markus et al., 2005). The OECD defines competencies more broadly and beyond mere knowledge and

skills: ‘[Competence] involves the ability to proficiently tackle complex challenges by utilising and deploying psy-

chosocial resources, encompassing both skills and attitudes, within a particular context’ (OECD,2005 , p. 4). In this

paper, we refer to OECD’ definition of ‘competence’.

Competencies in education, like in industrial enterprises, are often grouped under a competence framework or

model, which outlines essential skills and behaviours needed for effective performance in specific roles or educa-

tional contexts. Competency modelling is the activity of determining specific competencies that are characteristic

of high performance and success in a given job. Organisations adopting a competency approach create or utilise

a competency model, which at minimum consists of a simple list or catalogue specifying desirable competencies

(Markus et al., 2005).

Organisational competency models come in different types and structures, depending on the institutions'

objectives, values and belief system (Chouhan & Srivastava, 2014; Krajcovicova et al., 2012). Frameworks include

descriptions of competencies organised into tiers, making it easier for educators and professionals to understand

and apply them to their work. Various competency models have been created with diverse categorisation of com-

petencies: personal, social/interpersonal, action-­related and domain-­related, or grouping competencies into meta,

domain, method and social competencies. Additionally, there are competency models specifically designed for

leadership and management, as well as for particular tasks or job profiles (Prifti et al., 2017). Additionally, the mod-

els can be designed with two approaches: top-­down (by experts – competency modelling), bottom-­up (suggested

skills by people working in the field – job analysis). Competency modelling emphasises future roles aligned with

a strategic plan and defines optimal performance through worker attributes. In contrast, job analysis primarily

describes current work activities and establishes typical performance without addressing alignment issues within

the organisation (Stevens, 2013).

A competence framework makes a claim about its interrelated purpose, creating a position that requires vali-

dation. Experts examine claims by questioning them and gathering evidence (Child & Shaw, 2023). From an anal-

ysis of the competence framework literature, Child and Shaw (2023) identified four categories of claims in the

competence framework: Construct Claims, utility claims, audience claims and impact claims. In this review, we

focus on Competence Construct Claims of the competence frameworks, which are related to the definitions of

competencies ‘how elements are defined and labeled; how they combine to constitute overall competence; the

rational for defining and organizing things in this way rather than some other way’ (p. 31).

We searched for AI CFTs, studied them and noted the similarities among frameworks. Next, we categorised

them in a classification scheme within a Competence Construct Claims to provide a structured approach for un-

derstanding the definitions, labels and rationale of different AI CFTs: (1) Existing competence model-­oriented; (2)

Competence areas/domains-­oriented; (3) Process-­driven (process competency model); (4) AI systems-­driven (spe-

cific work/job model); and (5) Competence level-­driven (multidimensional). The framework categories N2, 3, 4 and

5 were found in the scientific literature (Klieme et al., 2003; Prifti et al., 2017; Rothwell & Lindholm, 1999). Below,

we will examine each of these types of AI CFT. First, we will explain the claim and the definition of the framework,

then, we will provide an example of the framework that falls into this category; and finally, we will summarise the

characteristics and strengths of each model.

In this category, we included Higuera's (2019) framework, who proposed five pillars or competencies that

should be added to teacher training in AI:

• Uncertainty and randomness – The acknowledgement of data inconsistency and the non-­causal nature of

AI-­generated forecasts in education. It underscores that AI predictions are estimations rather than absolute

truths. Embracing uncertainty implies teaching without assuming omniscience and accepting the reality that

mistakes can occur.

• Coding and computational thinking – The justification for including these lies in their prevalence in curricula

in many countries. Coding involves utilising programming language libraries, emphasising the importance of

coding skills. The argument is made that proficient AI users must comprehend algorithms to discern when it is

necessary not to rely blindly on machine learning decisions.

• Data awareness – Data and computing are at the centre of computing. Most effort will concern data

collection, visualisation, manipulation and analysis.

• Critical thinking – Achieving true technological literacy involves a fusion of comprehending digital concepts

and utilising algorithms to form informed opinions. The goal is to teach not just the use of technology but also to

impart the ability to understand its impacts, assess risks and cultivate a historical perspective on media.

• Post AI humanism – Impact on non-­technological attitudes, reconsider some ground truth: Truth, experience,

creativity and intelligence.

Higuera (2019) combined these five pillars with six aspects of the UNESCO ICT competence framework for

teachers (ICT-­CFT, UNESCO, 2018) by assessing the effectiveness of the ICT-­CFT as a framework for training

teachers in AI and measures that ICT-­CFT would benefit from the impact of AI. The relationship between pillars

and aspects is described in three ways: mutual impact, the impact of the pillar on the aspect and the influence of

an aspect on the pillar (see Table 2). The primary goal of the ICT CFT is to equip educators with hands-­on exper-

tise in effectively using and developing educational materials through ICT, which serves as the basis to integrate

understanding of computers, algorithms and data within AI.

Another important model, the Digital Competence Framework for Educators (2017), undergoes changes with

regard to AI. The EU Commission has added a new pivotal competence within this European framework: the eth-

ical utilisation of AI and data. For ethically employing AI systems, educators are required to understand essential

aspects such as human agency and oversight, transparency, diversity, non-­discrimination, fairness, societal and envi-

ronmental well-­being, privacy, data governance, technical robustness, safety and accountability. These elements are

included within the framework of digital competencies in the context of the European Framework for the Digital

Competence of Educators (Ethical Guidelines, 2021).

Lorenz and Romeike (2023) have systematically characterised teaching-­related AI competence areas by adapt-

ing the Digital Pedagogical and Content Knowledge (DPACK) model. They justify the incorporation of AI-­related

digital competencies within this framework, replacing the traditional ‘T’ for Technology with ‘D’ for Digital and

situating AI competencies within the Technology/Digital domain. The framework introduces three key compe-

tencies: AI-­K (Knowledge), AI-­PK (Pedagogical Knowledge), AI-­CK (Content Knowledge) and AI-­PCK (AI Related

Pedagogical Content Knowledge). AI-­K involves the ability to recognise, understand, reflect and shape AI phe-

nomena from technological, socio-­cultural, and user-­oriented perspectives. AI-­PK focuses on recognising and

reflecting on the potentials, limitations and risks of AI in teaching-­learning processes, facilitating the design of

contemporary educational settings. AI-­CK pertains to recognising and reflecting on the implications of increased

AI use in one's discipline, affecting the scientific field, professional domain and subject matter. AI-­PCK involves

recognising and reflecting on the subject-­specific influence, potentials and limitations of AI in teaching and learn-

ing processes and learners, guiding the design of modern educational settings. Based on the DPACK model, the

framework provides a structured description of AI education requirements.

While existing theoretical models such as TPACK and ICT CFT serve as valuable foundations for new com-

petence frameworks, it is essential to acknowledge that they may have certain rigidities that can limit the inte-

gration of innovative elements unless they are modified. Nevertheless, they provide a solid starting point for

researchers, educators and policymakers to develop comprehensive AI competence frameworks for educators.

3.2 | Competence areas/domains-­oriented (competency structured models)

Another common approach to developing a competence framework is to delineate the framework's areas by for-

mulating key questions or defining specific knowledge domains: What are the new knowledge areas? What are the

essential new skills or attitudes that educators need? These areas encompass mandatory skills for teachers and

various facets of education, ranging from classroom management to the ethical utilisation of AI, and they serve as

the primary focal points for policymakers and educators.

Olari and Romeike (2021) examined the relationship between AI and data literacy competence areas in ex-

isting educational frameworks and proposed a preliminary approach that utilises the data lifecycle to reflect on

data literacy competencies relevant to AI. They found that the available AI competence frameworks developed

for teacher education and schools do not include data literacy. To include data literacy, they proposed a novel

approach comprising eight primary competencies: (1) Extracting information from datasets and processing with

AI tools (Acquisition stage); (2) Conducting effective data cleaning and mitigating biases in datasets (Cleansing

stage); (3) Integrating learning processes and data management systems into practical tasks grounded in AI con-

cepts (Implementation stage); (4) Enhancing data through the application of AI techniques (Optimisation stage);

(5) Utilising AI algorithms to visualise data (Visualisation stage); (6) Generating new insights from data through

AI-­based analyses (Analysis stage); (7) Explaining results, including identifying errors and addressing them through

questioning (Evaluation stage); and (8) Employing AI-­driven procedures to enhance data archiving, deletion, or

sharing processes (Sharing, erasing and archiving stages).

develops education from pre-­school to higher education and beyond, strengthens education systems worldwide

and responds to contemporary global challenges through education. The UNESCO AI CFT has been designed to

complement and align with UNESCO's ICT Competency Framework for Teachers (ICT CFT) since 2018, draft-

ing a separate framework to address the challenges and opportunities presented by AI systems. The aim of this

framework is to guide teachers, educational communities and global teacher education systems in harnessing

3.3 | Process-­driven (process competency model) frameworks

The next group of frameworks are process-­driven or process competency models, which represent processes and

corresponding roles in a job. The representation of these models may have a complex design in relation to AIED.

To examine these frameworks, one could ask: What processes characterise AI application in the classroom? How

can these processes be translated into competencies? Or how can AI competencies attend to these processes?

Two conceptual frameworks on human-­AI hybrid approaches related to teachers' AI competencies are dis-

cussed in this section. The significance of conceptual frameworks is underscored by Antonenko (2015)), who

views them as practical tools for structuring inquiries. Conceptual frameworks are defined as a synthesis of be-

liefs, assumptions, theories and concepts that provide guidance throughout the inquiry process. Unlike relying

on existing theories or readily available models, a conceptual framework is meticulously constructed by the re-

searcher. Serving as a tool for integrating practical contexts, theoretical aspects and inquiry methods, it plays a

significant role in shaping the focus of inquiries or programmes. Importantly, conceptual frameworks are not rigid

formulas but rather offer guidance for curriculum and program design tailored to specific contexts (Falloon, 2020).

The field of AIED introduces new responsibilities for both learners and teachers, necessitating teachers’ at-

tention, decision-­making, action and observation. Holstein et al. (2020) explored a conceptual framework for the

process of human-­AI hybrid adaptivity in education. They investigated a set of essential components, including

goals/targets, perception, action, decision policies and granularity/timing. These components examine how hu-

mans and AI systems can complement each other in distinctive ways:

1.	 Goal Augmentation: In this aspect, both the teacher and AI system collaborate to enhance their respective

objectives, striving to achieve superior outcomes

2.	 Perceptual Augmentation: This component focuses on enhancing perceptual abilities. Both the human teacher

and AI system work to improve their capacity to sense and understand information

3.	 Action Augmentation: Here, both the teacher and AI system strengthen their capabilities to take action or ex-

ecute tasks more effectively

4.	 Decision Augmentation: This part concentrates on refining the decision-­making process. The teacher and AI

system collaborate to make better decisions by sharing their insights and expertise.

5.	 Granularity and Timing: The frequency or granularity at which the perception-­action cycle is performed varies,

ranging from once per task/step to once per conversation turn/ design iteration.

3.4 | AI-­systems (specific job/work model) frameworks

Another approach to organise essential AI competencies for teachers involves grouping them around a job/work

model (AI systems). Recent research has explored machine working frameworks that suggest categorising skills

based on the specific AI models in use. These frameworks typically comprise three key components: machine

operational competencies, user competencies and utilisation competencies. Correspondingly, the questions to

analyse such frameworks are the following: What knowledge is necessary for the AI system operation? How this

knowledge be applied in practice? Who are the users of this system? How should this system operation adhere to

regulations of data safety, privacy and protection?

For instance, Carolus et al. (2023) conducted a study aimed at defining competencies for effective interac-

tions with voice-­based AI systems, addressing a notable gap in AI-­related literacy models. Developers, designers

and similar profession experts who self-­reportedly developed or designed language-­based AI systems in their

work and have gained relevant experience in this area were interviewed. The answers resulted in the creation

of a comprehensive Digital Interaction Literacy (DIL) competency model, which encompasses both universal

and specific skills tailored to different user groups, including parents, teachers and developers. The DIL Model

places a strong emphasis on enhancing user proficiency by fostering a foundational understanding of AI systems,

including their operations and natural language processing capabilities. This, in turn, empowers users to make

well-­informed decisions and optimise their interactions with these systems. The primary objective of the DIL

Model is to encourage conscientious engagement with AI systems by promoting awareness of both their benefits

and challenges. This model equips practitioners with a guide for considering the perspectives of diverse users,

allowing them to align their expectations with reality, extract more significant benefits from their interactions

with AI systems and adapt their behaviour, such as by providing instructions for formulating appropriate re-

quests (Table 6).

AI competence frameworks tailored to specific AI systems can be particularly beneficial for educators at

the early stages of AI introduction to schools and for those who are seeking to master narrow AI competen-

cies. The introduction of AI into education is a gradual process that depends on country and school policy,

and such frameworks can be highly effective when educators work with specific AI models (e.g., application of

ChatGPT in Su & Yang, 2023) introduced in their schools. Such AI competence frameworks can complement

3.5 | Competence level-­driven frameworks

As AI continues to gain prominence in educational settings, the development of new AI competence frameworks

becomes an evolving effort. To meet the emerging needs of AI integration in schools, an innovative competence-­

level-­driven approach is being adopted. This approach is designed to seamlessly weave together various levels of

competencies. It forges a comprehensive link between AI models, tools, applications, implications and attitudes,

thus creating a multidimensional framework for AI competence and answering the following questions: What

levels does this model consist of? Are there sub-­competencies? Are AI competencies divided into components?

Sattelmaier and Pawlowski (2023) have introduced a Generative AI (GAI) competence framework tailored

for K-­12 schools, with a primary focus on equipping both teachers and students. This framework unfolds across

multiple tiers, beginning with general, digital and domain-­specific competencies, which include fundamental skills

like critical thinking and data literacy. It progresses into more advanced AI competencies, comprising algorithms,

programming, data literacy, AI techniques, AI technologies, AI development, application of AI, ethics of AI and so-

cial implications. Moreover, this framework introduces emerging competencies focusing on the art of effectively

prompting large language models. This involves an understanding of different GAI model types, responsible util-

isation of GAI tools and applications for content generation, thorough exploration of GAI implications, including

risk assessment and problem-­solving, and the development of AI attitudes, linked to the broader implications of

AI (Figure 1).

Another example of competence level-­driven framework is Ng et al. (2023) framework. The framework in-

cludes: technical and non-­technical skills, and meaningful strategies to enhance students' AI competencies. The

model suggests that proficiency in AI should extend beyond technical aspects (e.g., attitudes, skills and knowl-

edge). It advocates for a comprehensive understanding that acknowledges a non-­technical, critical competence

that young people should acquire to ethically, safely and wisely navigate AI technologies. Taking a broader per-

spective, teachers are encouraged not to perceive AI competence as solely a distinct technological area but as

an opportunity to cultivate other non-­technical skills, including life and career skills, multidisciplinary skills and

learning and innovation skills. Additionally, it presents a revised model for teachers to implement meaningful

strategies for enhancing their students' AI competencies through four key processes: (1) teachers' professional

engagement; (2) instructional support; (3) content choices across disciplines; and (4) students' learning competen-

cies (Ng et al., 2023).

The competence level-­driven approach is a systematic endeavour, providing depth, clarity and hierarchy to

the relationships and connections within the framework, which might pose questions about its implementa-

tion in practice. As authors claim, such models are not complete because they cannot include all evolving tools

(Sattelmaier & Pawlowski, 2023). It portrays the construction of competencies as blocks, layers or levels, offering

an illustration of the complex nature of teacher education in the age of AI. With such a profound representation of

competencies, prioritisation of competencies and consultations with experts could be important.

4 | DISCUSSION

The objective of this paper was to explore AI Competence Frameworks for Teachers (AI CFTs) and classify them

within Competence Construct Claims. We provided a structured classification of available AI CFTs to under-

stand their foci and characteristics, including AI competencies and the rationale behind their grouping to promote

teacher professional development in AI (Table 7).

In our critical review on AI CFTs, we distinguished the following types of frameworks: (1) Existing competence

model-­oriented; (2) Competence areas/domains-­oriented; (3) Process-­driven (process competency model); () AI

systems-­driven (specific work/job model) and (5) Competence level-­driven (multidimensional). All the frameworks

under discussion were at the initial stage of their development. Due to the diverse and complex nature of arti-

ficial intelligence, the models were inherently intricate and required thoughtful consideration. It is remarkable

that the frameworks that were devised from empirical data – bottom-­up approach (Carolus et al., 2023; Kim &

Kwon, 2023) – are more detailed and include descriptions of competencies at the micro level, and the frameworks

produced from conceptual models are more general with broader areas (Higuera, 2019; Lorenz & Romeike, 2023).

Nevertheless, all the frameworks need validation and real-­life application, including investigation and the collec-

tion of evidence (Table 7).

Our classification of AI Competence Frameworks (CFTs) can be instrumental for researchers and policymakers

in comprehending approaches to teacher competence development. Defining teachers' AI competence necessi-

tates a comprehensive mapping, considering both top-­down and bottom-­up perspectives.

While policy-­makers, researchers and app designers may contribute to a top-­down approach in delineating

teachers' AI competencies, the empirical and design-­based nature of AIED requires a solid theoretical foundation.

The adoption of theoretical frameworks serves as a unifying force, fostering shared concepts and terminology

among researchers and designers, facilitating more efficient communication and innovation (Graziani et al., 2023).

Holstein et al. (2020) emphasise the significant influence of theory in guiding the formulation of hypotheses, pre-

dictions, comprehension and design strategies within AIED. However, a notable disparity arises, as most AI com-

petencies are derived from instructional theories or literature reviews rather than teachers' actual experiences.

Consequently, teachers often perceive these proposed competencies as irrelevant to enhancing their teaching

practice, leading to diminished motivation (Kim & Kwon, 2023).

On the other hand, it is essential that, through empirical studies, AI teachers' competencies are defined (bot-

tom-­up): what components/elements they comprise, which cognitive, action-­related or motivational attributes

they embrace. With the wide range of AI-­tools, it is important to understand what kind of problems AI teachers'

competencies will solve and what kind of solutions AI teachers' competencies convey. Another important ques-

tion is how these competencies are developed, assessed or measured (Klieme et al., 2003). Further research is

vital to assess the practical application of AI tools and techniques. Without first-­hand experience of these tools

and systems in action, it is challenging to predict the precise processes and skills needed for their smooth opera-

tion. By blending top-­down expertise with bottom-­up input, AI competency models can become more dynamic,

relevant and adaptable to the evolving realities of the field.

As the field of AIED continues to evolve, several concerns should be addressed regarding the development

of AI CFTs. First, it is imperative to acknowledge that AI extends beyond the realm of traditional digital technol-

ogy. Educators' competencies should be well-­coordinated to prevent redundancy and minimise potential confu-

sion if AI competencies are infused in Digital Competence Frameworks for Educators. Additionally, the Digital

Competence Frameworks establish specific target levels that educators are expected to attain. This raises a per-

tinent question: should AI CFTs also encompass prerequisites, mastery levels and a successive progression of

proficiency or competency benchmarks to ensure educators' proficiency aligns with the demands of AI-­enhanced

education? Furthermore, understanding how educators are assessed for their AI competencies and how they can

assess their students' AI literacy is crucial.

Second, the intersection of AI and subject didactics needs a thoughtful examination. Presently, AI competen-

cies are often framed as cross-­curricular, transcending specific subjects. However, an evolving discourse proposes

the inclusion of subject-­specific AI competencies. This prompts a reflection on the necessity for these compe-

tency models to be underpinned by the theoretical framework of subject didactics. Educators must possess deep

subject knowledge in addition to digital and AI competencies to effectively guide their students in domain specific

learning. Knowledge on the integration of AI in teaching and learning is generally limited within the industry

(Havinga & Clary, 2023). In a curriculum landscape often dominated by large publishers and app designers, en-

gagement of educators in curriculum development is therefore vital. The right balance between subject expertise

and AI proficiency is a challenge that must be addressed in AI CFTs.

Third, AI CFTs should include a specific implementation model which will ensure the stages of AI adoption,

adaptation, appropriation, transformation, mastery and optimisation depending on the specific technology, con-

text and educational objectives. Furthermore, resources required to implement AI competence frameworks in

educational institutions is essential. This includes considerations of funding, infrastructure, teacher training and

ongoing support.

Finally, it is essential to recognise that there is no perfect AI CFT. As these frameworks evolve, their effec-

tiveness depends on the extent of AI knowledge considered necessary for teachers to acquire for the application

of AI in education. The ongoing quest for the ideal AI competence framework should be driven by the vision of

educators' AI proficiency. As AI competence areas continue to evolve, it is imperative to facilitate interdisciplinary

discussions involving experts and subject teachers. AI CFTs should be improved and implemented to ensure that

educators are equipped with a versatile combination of pedagogy, subject expertise, digital literacy and AI profi-

ciency, ultimately empowering them to provide technologically enriched education for their students.

5 | CONCLUSION

This paper explored AI Competence Frameworks for Teachers (AI CFTs) and classified them within the Competence

Construct Claims. While this classification into five distinct types reveals the diverse nature of existing frame-

works, it also underscores the early stage of AI CFT development and the complexity of addressing the multi-

faceted aspects of AI. Future research is recommended to focus on the validation of frameworks, longitudinal

analyses and assessments of AI CFTs to gauge the real-­life impact, sustained effectiveness and global applicability

of the frameworks. Additionally, investigating specific implementation models and including stages of AI adoption

will contribute to refining and effectively implementing AI CFTs.

The review covers AI CFTs in scientific papers between 2019 and 2023 and may not reflect the most recent

developments or perspectives, especially in the rapidly changing AI field. We acknowledge that information and

understanding of a topic can evolve over time and we may unintentionally bring our own biases or preconceived

notions to the review process, influencing the interpretation of the material.
